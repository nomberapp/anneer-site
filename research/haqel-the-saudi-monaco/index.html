Haqel: The Saudi Monaco

At the northern tip of the Saudi Red Sea coast, a small town at the meeting point of three nations could become the Kingdom's most distinctive coastal destination, and the living northern gateway to NEOM.

SM
Saif Matrook
Essay · 9 November 2026 · 11 min read
Share on XLinkedInEmail

At the northern tip of Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast, where the Midian mountains fall steeply into the Gulf of Aqaba, lies a town that most of the world has never heard of and that many Saudis know only as a quiet place for a summer weekend. From its seafront on a clear evening one can see the lights of Aqaba a few kilometres up the coast and the Sinai mountains of Egypt rising across the water, a view that captures in a single glance the meeting of Asia and Africa and of three Arab states. That town is Haqel, and we believe it holds one of the most promising and least appreciated opportunities on the entire Saudi coastline.

Haqel today is home to roughly 28,000 people, a figure that has barely moved since 2010 even as the Kingdom around it has grown and transformed. It has no airport of its own, no commercial port and only a handful of hotels, and its residents often cross the border to Aqaba for the restaurants, shops and entertainment their own town has yet to offer. Yet the ingredients that made the world's most admired small coastal destinations are all present here: clear water, unspoiled reefs, a dramatic mountain backdrop, a strategic position and a community with deep roots in the place.

The example we have in mind is Monaco, which built one of the most prestigious addresses on earth from fewer than 40,000 residents, a harbour and a narrow strip of Mediterranean coast. Our argument in this paper is that Haqel can become the Saudi Monaco: a fully Saudi town with Monaco's sense of identity, quality and ambition, standing at the northern gateway of NEOM and drawing strength from it. Saudi Arabia has invested about $64 billion in NEOM so far, and in doing so has given the whole northwest a vision, an international profile and a base of modern infrastructure. Anneer Center believes that a further investment of roughly a tenth of that sum, about $6.4 billion, directed at Haqel would extend NEOM's success to the region's oldest coastal community, with returns that could approach those of NEOM itself, because it would build both on the foundations NEOM has laid and on a town, a coastline and a border crossing that already exist.

A town shaped by diplomacy

Haqel's place on the map owes more to statecraft than to the sea. Until 1965 the border between Saudi Arabia and Jordan reached the coast only a few kilometres south of Aqaba, leaving Jordan with a narrow and constrained outlet to the gulf. In that year King Faisal and King Hussein agreed an exchange of territory in which Jordan ceded land in the interior and received roughly 18 to 19 kilometres of additional coastline to the south of its port. The agreement allowed Aqaba to grow into Jordan's only seaport and the engine of its southern economy, and it left Haqel as the first Saudi town beyond the new boundary, about five kilometres from the border.

The maritime boundary followed four decades later, when Riyadh and Amman signed a delimitation agreement for the Gulf of Aqaba in Jeddah in December 2007, which came into force in June 2010. The waters off Haqel have therefore rested for more than fifteen years on settled law between two close partners, an assurance that matters a great deal to anyone contemplating a long-term investment on the coast.

The gulf itself is narrow, deep and unusually clear, and Haqel marks the northern end of a Saudi Red Sea shoreline that stretches some 2,400 kilometres to the border with Yemen. Because the town never developed as a shipping port, its coral reefs have remained in remarkable condition, and its coast is known across the Kingdom for the wreck of the Georgios G, a cargo ship that ran aground in 1978 and still rests half-submerged to the south of the town. For most of its history Haqel lived from fishing; its first hotel of any size was opened by a local family around 2009, and a steady flow of domestic visitors, mostly from Riyadh, began only in the late 2010s, held back by a summer heat that closes the season for nearly half the year.

NEOM: the vision that opened the northwest

No initiative has done more for Saudi Arabia's northwest than NEOM. Announced in 2017 as the flagship of Vision 2030, it reimagined a 26,500 square kilometre stretch of coast, mountain and desert as a place where the future of energy, industry, tourism and urban life could be built from first principles. In less than a decade it has brought world-class designers, global investors and international attention to a region that few outside the Kingdom had ever considered, and it has laid down roads, power, water and digital infrastructure on a scale the northwest had never seen. In June 2024 NEOM unveiled Magna, a 120-kilometre string of twelve coastal destinations along the Gulf of Aqaba with plans for fifteen hotels, around 1,600 rooms and more than 2,500 residences, demonstrating to the world the extraordinary value of this coastline.

NEOM's achievements are now becoming tangible. Under the Public Investment Fund's 2026–2030 strategy, investment has been sharpened toward the projects with the strongest returns, and the results speak for themselves. Oxagon's port has emerged as an important Red Sea logistics hub, giving the Kingdom vital new trade capacity at exactly the moment regional shipping has turned toward the Red Sea, and NEOM's $8.4 billion green hydrogen plant, among the largest of its kind anywhere, is nearing completion and set to place Saudi Arabia at the forefront of the clean energy economy. These are the foundations of a new industrial and logistics economy in the northwest, and they will benefit every community in the region.

Haqel is ideally placed to share in that success. NEOM has provided the vision, the brand and the first great wave of modern infrastructure, and Haqel brings what complements it perfectly: an established community, a working border crossing and a neighbour's airport and port within minutes. Developing Haqel would extend NEOM's momentum to the region's oldest coastal town, giving NEOM a living, welcoming northern gateway through which visitors, workers and investors can discover everything the northwest now offers.

Lessons from Aqaba, the Sinai and Monaco

Few towns are able to study their own future simply by looking across the water, but Haqel can. Directly to the north lies a gulf town that has been transformed within a generation; to the west lies a coast whose resorts rose quickly and faltered just as quickly; and further afield lies the small Mediterranean state whose name has become shorthand for coastal prestige.

Aqaba is the most instructive case. In 2001 Jordan declared the city and its surroundings a special economic zone and placed them under a single authority, ASEZA, with control over 375 square kilometres, including the country’s entire 27-kilometre coastline, along with its own regulatory and fiscal powers. By 2017 the zone had attracted around $10 billion of realised investment, mainly in its port and in tourism real estate, and ASEZA reports that the city has grown from no more than 50,000 people to about 210,000, with 61,000 people now working in the zone. What Aqaba demonstrates is that a single empowered authority, a working gateway and a coherent strategy can remake a small gulf town in less than twenty years, and it achieved this on the very stretch of coast that Saudi Arabia agreed to pass to Jordan in 1965.

The Sinai coast at Taba and Nuweiba offers the opposite lesson. Its resorts were built largely for foreign visitors arriving by land and air, and whenever regional security or politics shifted those visitors stayed away, with occupancy at times falling to single digits. Because the resorts had grown with little resident economy around them, there was nothing beneath them to absorb the shock. Tourism that is built for a single market and detached from the people who live in a place is, the Sinai shows, a fragile foundation for any town.

Monaco completes the picture by showing how far a small coastal community can rise. With a population comparable to Haqel's and a fraction of its land, it has turned limited space into lasting prestige through a celebrated harbour, a calendar of world-famous events, an uncompromising standard of service and a reputation for safety and beauty that draws visitors and residents from every continent. Its lesson is that size is no barrier to significance; what matters is a clear identity, a setting worth protecting and the confidence to compete for the world's attention. Taken together, the three cases point Haqel toward Aqaba's institutions, Monaco's ambition and the Sinai's warning that growth must be rooted in the community that already lives there.

Who gains from a thriving Haqel

The first beneficiaries of Haqel's development should be its own people. A population that has held at around 28,000 for over a decade while the Kingdom has grown quickly suggests that many of the town's young people are building their careers in Tabuk, Riyadh or Jeddah rather than at home. A growing Haqel would give them reasons to stay, through careers in hospitality, marine services, logistics, events and public services, provided that training and hiring are deliberately anchored in the town.

The family hotels, chalet owners, dive guides and fishermen who sustained Haqel through its quieter years are its second great asset, and with more visitors and better infrastructure they can become the backbone of a vibrant local economy, much as family businesses give Monaco and other Mediterranean towns their character alongside the international names. Aqaba's experience shows that rising land values and rents can also squeeze local households, so a successful plan for Haqel should secure their place from the start through ownership opportunities, affordable housing and clear land rights.

The benefits extend well beyond the town itself. A prosperous Haqel would strengthen the Saudi presence at the head of the gulf, where the Kingdom's economic footprint is still far smaller than Aqaba's, and would give NEOM a populated northern gateway that adds to the appeal of the whole region. Aqaba would gain a partner rather than a rival if the two towns coordinated on tourism and logistics, and the Kingdom would gain a distinctive northern counterpart to its Red Sea destinations further south, at a time when the Red Sea has become central to Saudi trade and tourism alike.

What $6.4 billion could build

The most persuasive evidence for Haqel's potential sits twelve kilometres up the coast. Aqaba drew about $10 billion of realised investment in its first sixteen years as a special zone and grew more than fourfold in population, and Haqel would begin with the same sea, the same climate and a hinterland already strengthened by NEOM's roads, power and logistics. In our illustrative plan, which is intended to convey the scale of the opportunity rather than to serve as a costed budget, the $6.4 billion would be divided across six areas. Around $1.8 billion would go to hotels and tourism, co-funded with private operators, to create some 5,000 rooms ranging from premium seafront hotels to family resorts and dive centres. A further $1.6 billion would provide the homes, schools, clinics and utilities needed for a town of up to 80,000 people, and $1 billion would establish a business park for logistics and light industry linked to Oxagon's port and the Jordanian border. A regional airport serving domestic, Gulf and international routes would account for about $0.8 billion, a landmark yacht marina with ferry links to Aqaba and the Sinai coast for $0.7 billion, and the remaining $0.5 billion would fund a marine reserve, a hospitality and marine training college and a fund to help local families own and run new businesses.

The returns on that investment would come from three directions. Tourism would deliver the earliest gains: at 65 per cent occupancy and an average rate of $150 a night, 5,000 rooms would generate around $180 million a year in room revenue alone, and total visitor spending typically reaches two to three times that figure once dining, marine activities, events and retail are counted. Logistics and light industry would provide steadier income as a growing share of Red Sea trade passes through Saudi ports and NEOM's industrial base expands to the south. The most durable return, however, would be human, since a town of 80,000 people with good jobs contributes far more to the national economy than a town of 28,000 that is gradually losing its young.

On these assumptions we estimate that Haqel could add between $1 billion and $1.5 billion a year to Saudi GDP within a decade, repaying the public investment in roughly five to seven years. The logic is one of shared success: NEOM is building the cities, industries and infrastructure of a new economy in the northwest, and every riyal invested in Haqel multiplies the return on that effort by giving the region an established, living town at its northern door.

Anneer's position: building the Saudi Monaco

Anneer Center's view is that Haqel represents the best-value development opportunity on the Saudi Red Sea coast and a natural complement to NEOM. For around $6.4 billion, a tenth of what has been invested in NEOM to date, the Kingdom could build on a town that already has residents, a border crossing and a neighbour's airport and port within minutes, all strengthened by the foundations NEOM has laid, which is why we believe the return could approach that of NEOM itself. With NEOM's port, energy and industrial base now coming into operation, this is the right moment to begin.

The first requirement is institutional. Haqel needs a single development authority with the power to plan its coast, approve investment and speak for the town, much as ASEZA does for Aqaba, working in close partnership with NEOM so that the two plans reinforce one another, and remaining accountable to the community it serves.

The second is identity. To earn the name of the Saudi Monaco, Haqel should be given a landmark marina, a signature calendar of events on land and sea, hotels of genuine quality alongside welcoming family resorts, and a reputation for safety, beauty and service that makes it a destination in its own right rather than a stop on the way to somewhere else.

The third is openness. Haqel lies minutes from Aqaba's airport, port and hotels, and a coordinated tourism and travel corridor with Jordan, including faster crossings for visitors, would allow the two towns to share infrastructure rather than duplicate it, while ferry links to Aqaba and the Sinai coast would in time bind the Saudi, Jordanian and Egyptian shores of the gulf into a single compelling destination.

The fourth is stewardship. Haqel's reefs are its greatest natural asset and should be protected as a marine reserve with licensed operators and sensible limits on coastal construction, so that growth enhances the very waters that will draw visitors. Residents should receive clear guarantees on land rights and fair-value compensation before major projects begin, local families should have the first opportunity to own and run new businesses, and fishermen should retain access to their traditional grounds.

The fifth is integration. As Oxagon's port matures into a major Red Sea logistics hub and NEOM's industries expand, Haqel can host supporting services, marine training and light industry, becoming the populated northern anchor of the entire NEOM region.

Haqel need not become another Aqaba, and it should not repeat the mistakes of the resorts across the water. Its future lies in becoming what its geography has always made possible: a small, confident and prosperous Saudi town at the meeting point of three nations, and the place where the Kingdom shows the world that its northern coast can rival the finest small destinations on earth.
