Just Nationalism: Pride Without Superiority

Nationalism is neither good nor bad in itself. What matters is whether national pride drives governments to compete in raising the quality of life of those they govern, or to compete in exclusion.

AC
Anneer Center
Analysis · 10 November 2026 · 13 min read
Share on XLinkedInEmail

Introduction

Nationalism has returned to the centre of world politics. From sovereignty campaigns in Europe to development drives in Asia and the Gulf, governments and movements once again speak openly in the language of the nation: its interests, its heritage, its place in the world. The response from much of the international policy community has been alarm. Nationalism, the argument goes, is the force that drove the wars of the twentieth century, that turned neighbours against one another in the Balkans and elsewhere, and that today threatens the cooperation a crowded planet needs to manage climate, trade and disease.

That warning deserves to be taken seriously. Yet it rests on an assumption that is rarely examined: that nationalism is a single thing, and that its dangers are built into it. The historical record suggests otherwise. The same sentiment that has fuelled exclusion and conflict has also powered anti-colonial liberation, held together fragile multi-ethnic states, and pushed governments to build schools, hospitals and infrastructure for people they regard as their own. Nationalism has been both a source of cruelty and a source of collective improvement. The question is not whether nations should take pride in themselves, but what keeps that pride honest.

This analysis proposes an answer, which we call just nationalism: pride in a nation's culture, values and achievements, held in check by justice and equal treatment for everyone who lives under its authority. Just nationalism accepts that people love their homelands and that this love can be a powerful engine of public good. It rejects the moment at which that love becomes a claim of superiority over others. Our argument is that this line can be drawn clearly, that one of the world's great moral traditions drew it long ago, and that any country can be measured against it.

Where Nationalism Goes Wrong

Critics of nationalism raise many objections: that it excludes minorities, that it breeds conflict, that it weakens international cooperation, that it presses individuals into a single collective identity, that it tempts governments towards protectionism. These are real concerns. But on closer inspection most of them grow from a single root.

Every nationalism must answer the question of who belongs. In doing so it draws a line between “us” and “them.” In itself, that line is neither sinister nor avoidable; every political community has members, and every state owes particular duties to its citizens. The danger lies in what happens next. Pride in “us” can gradually become a belief that “we” are better than “them,” and once that shift occurs, those outside the line, or those inside it who think differently, begin to be treated as less deserving of rights, dignity or a hearing. Exclusion, discrimination and, at the extreme, violence follow from that belief, not from the love of homeland that preceded it.

This shift rarely happens all at once. Few nationalist movements begin as openly racist projects. More often the drift is gradual and arrives under pressure: an economic downturn, a security threat, a rivalry with a neighbour, or a political leader who finds it useful to stir resentment. Nationalism runs on emotion, and justice requires restraint. It is precisely in moments of crisis, when emotions run highest, that restraint is hardest to keep.

This is also why the distinction often drawn by political scientists between “civic” and “ethnic” nationalism, while useful, is incomplete. Civic nationalism, built on shared citizenship and values, is generally considered the safer form, and ethnic nationalism, built on descent, the more dangerous. But civic nationalisms can also turn exclusionary when citizenship itself becomes a privilege to be guarded against outsiders. What matters is not only the basis on which a nation defines itself, but how it treats those it does not count as its own.

A Line Drawn Long Ago

The tension between love of one's people and justice towards others is not new. Islamic tradition addressed it fourteen centuries ago, and the distinction it drew remains one of the clearest available.

On one side, Islam accepts attachment to the homeland as natural and good. When the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) was forced to leave Mecca, he is reported to have addressed the city as the most beloved land to him, saying he would not have left had its people not driven him out. Love of one's land and people is not treated as a moral failing. (A popular saying, “love of the homeland is part of faith,” is often attributed to the Prophet (PBUH), though scholars regard that attribution as unreliable; the principle, however, is well supported elsewhere.)

On the other side, Islam firmly rejects what the Arabic tradition calls asabiyya: blind partisanship for one's own group, supporting it whether it is right or wrong. The Prophet (PBUH) is reported to have said that whoever calls to such partisanship is not one of us, and when asked what it meant, he described it as helping one's people in wrongdoing. The condemnation is not of loving one's people, but of placing that love above justice.

The Qur'an supplies the principle that separates the two: “O mankind, We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most righteous of you” (49:13). National and tribal identities are affirmed as part of human diversity, yet they confer no rank. In his Farewell Sermon, the Prophet (PBUH) made the point explicit: no Arab has superiority over a non-Arab, nor a non-Arab over an Arab, except through righteousness. And the tradition offers a simple test for how to treat others: none of you truly believes until he wants for his brother what he wants for himself.

Muslim scholars have long drawn on these principles to distinguish between wataniyya, love of and loyalty to the homeland, and qawmiyya understood as nationalism grounded in ethnic or racial superiority. Views differ: some scholars reject modern nationalism altogether as a division of the wider Muslim community, while others accept patriotism within these limits. But the core distinction is widely shared, and it maps closely onto the problem identified above. Pride in one's nation is legitimate; superiority over others is not.

This matters beyond the Muslim world. The Islamic distinction is one expression of a standard many moral traditions share, and it has the advantage of precision. It does not ask people to stop loving their nations. It asks them to measure worth by conduct rather than by birth, and to want for others the dignity and fairness they want for themselves. That is the standard just nationalism adopts.

Testing the Standard

A standard is only useful if it can be applied. The cases below are not a ranking, and no country meets the standard in full. Each was chosen because it illuminates a different part of the test: whether national pride sits alongside equal treatment, whether it drives governments to improve the lives of the governed, and how it handles those outside the national circle.

Singapore: pride built on equality

Singapore is perhaps the closest modern example of the balance just nationalism describes. A small state that became independent in 1965 amid ethnic tension, it chose to build its national identity on shared citizenship rather than on the culture of its largest community. Citizens of Chinese, Malay and Indian descent are equal in law, the state deliberately mixes communities in public housing to prevent segregation, and national pride is tied closely to performance: the quality of its schools, its public services and its economy. Singaporean nationalism is, in large part, a competition with the rest of the world to deliver a better life.

The weaknesses are equally instructive. Political opposition and public criticism operate within tight limits, and the large population of low-wage migrant workers, many housed in dormitories apart from the rest of society, does not share in the equality citizens enjoy. Singapore shows that pride and equality can coexist among citizens; it also shows that the hardest test of just nationalism lies at the edge of citizenship.

Japan: cultural pride, closed doors

Japan demonstrates how deeply a nation can preserve its culture and values while achieving very high standards of living. Its sense of national identity is strong, its social cohesion is remarkable, and it has managed modernisation without abandoning tradition. In this respect it embodies the first half of just nationalism: pride in heritage as a source of public good.

Yet Japan has long kept its doors comparatively closed. Foreign residents remain a small share of the population, naturalisation is demanding, and long-settled communities such as ethnic Koreans have historically faced discrimination. As its population ages and shrinks, Japan is cautiously opening to foreign workers. How it treats them will test whether its national pride can extend fairness to those who were not born into it.

Hungary and India: when identity narrows

Hungary and India, very different countries, illustrate the drift that just nationalism warns against. In both, nationalism has increasingly been defined around a single cultural or religious identity. Hungary's government has framed the nation in explicitly Christian and ethnic terms, set itself against immigration, and described its model as an “illiberal” democracy. In India, the governing movement's vision of the nation draws on Hindu identity, and measures such as the 2019 Citizenship Amendment Act, which offered a faster path to citizenship to migrants from neighbouring countries but excluded Muslims, have drawn criticism for treating faith as a condition of belonging.

Supporters in both countries argue that they are protecting national culture against outside pressure, and that argument is not without force. But both cases show the mechanism described earlier: when pride in a national identity comes to define who fully belongs, those outside that identity, including fellow citizens, risk becoming lesser members of their own nation.

Rwanda: rebuilding a shared nation

Rwanda offers a case of nationalism used to repair rather than divide. After the 1994 genocide, in which ethnic identity was weaponised to devastating effect, the state set out to build a single Rwandan identity. Ethnic labels were removed from identity documents, and national programmes promoted the idea of being Rwandan first. Combined with a strong focus on development, this has helped produce one of Africa's more stable and fastest-improving societies.

The Rwandan case also carries a warning. The same national project has been accompanied by tight restrictions on political opposition and public debate, sometimes justified as protection against renewed division. Rwanda shows that nationalism can heal; it also shows how easily the language of unity can be used to silence legitimate criticism.

The Role of Criticism

If the central danger of nationalism is a gradual drift from pride into superiority, then any just nationalism needs a way of noticing that drift before it is too late. That mechanism is criticism. Citizens, journalists and scholars who can speak openly are the ones who raise the alarm when minorities or foreign residents are treated unfairly, when officials exploit the national cause, or when development promises go unmet. Pride that cannot be questioned soon becomes propaganda, and a government that hears no criticism competes less effectively to improve the lives of its people.

This does not mean speech without limits. Lies that damage a person's reputation, incitement to violence and hatred directed at groups are restricted in almost every society, and rightly so. The difficulty lies in separating fair criticism from falsehood or speech driven by hostile agendas. Our view is that the test should fall on what is said rather than on who says it. Motives are almost impossible to prove, and criticism from those with an agenda can still be true, while well-intentioned critics can be wrong. The questions that matter are whether a claim is true or made in good faith, whether it is specific enough to be checked, and whether it argues or incites.

Who applies that test matters as much as the test itself. If the institution being criticised decides what counts as an illegitimate agenda, the label will expand to cover any criticism it dislikes. Independent courts, a clear burden of proof on those who claim to have been wronged, and remedies such as correction and a right of reply offer a sturdier safeguard. Often the most effective answer to a false claim is not to ban it, which can make it appear true, but to answer it openly with facts.

The law is not the only boundary. The 2026 Ohio governor's race offered a reminder: a campaign advertisement featuring a candidate's estranged mother, lawful under American free-speech protections, provoked a bipartisan backlash and was eventually withdrawn, and the candidate who aired it lost. Society enforced a standard of decency that the law did not. Just nationalism relies on both layers: law to restrain lies and harm, and a shared culture of values, what the Islamic tradition calls akhlaq, or good character, to restrain cruelty. Islamic tradition also supports open criticism directly, through the duty of nasiha, sincere counsel, including to those in authority, and the principle of shura, consultation.

Answering the Critics

A standard that claims to tame nationalism must face its strongest opponents. Four objections deserve a direct answer.

“Just nationalism is a contradiction.” Nationalism, critics argue, means putting one's own nation first, while justice means treating all people equally; a just nationalism is therefore universal ethics wearing a flag. The objection mistakes the relationship between the two. Justice does not require that people feel the same attachment to every community on earth, any more than it requires parents to love every child as they love their own. It requires that attachment never license wrongdoing towards others. National loyalty supplies something abstract principles struggle to supply on their own: the motivation to build, sacrifice and pay taxes for a shared home. Justice is not a replacement for that loyalty but its limit. This is precisely the structure of the Islamic distinction: love of one's people is affirmed, and supporting them in wrongdoing is condemned.

“Every nationalism starts out just.” The exclusionary movements of recent decades did not announce their intentions; they began with pride and culture. If the drift is so common, critics ask, what actually stops it? The honest answer is that no principle stops it on its own. What slows the drift is institutional: equal citizenship written into law, courts able to rule against governments, a free press, and the protection of minorities written into rules that a passing majority cannot easily change. Where nationalist governments weaken those institutions, the drift has already begun, and that weakening is itself the clearest early warning. Just nationalism therefore treats these safeguards not as optional features but as the condition on which national pride is legitimate.

“Fairness to non-citizens is too soft a demand.” If non-citizens may hold different rights from citizens, critics argue, the door is open to a permanent underclass. The concern is serious. Every state distinguishes citizens from visitors, and some difference in rights is unavoidable. But difference cannot be unlimited. A just nationalism guarantees every resident the same basic protections against abuse, the same access to justice and fair pay for work done, and it offers those who build their lives in the country, and especially those born and raised there, a realistic path to full membership. A nation that depends on people it will never allow to belong has not met the standard.

“Competition between nations is not always benign.” Nations compete not only in raising living standards but in military power, in lowering wages and protections to attract capital, and in drawing skilled workers away from poorer countries. The objection is correct as a description of what nationalism can do, which is why the standard specifies the competition it values: competition measured by the quality of life a state delivers to those it governs, not by the harm it inflicts on rivals. A race to the bottom on workers' rights or a contest in armaments fails the test by its own terms.

None of these answers makes nationalism safe by definition. They make it accountable: they turn a sentiment into a set of conditions that can be observed, measured and, where they are not met, criticised.

Conclusion: The Test of a Just Nationalism

Nationalism is neither good nor bad in itself. Its value depends on what it drives governments and societies to do. When national pride pushes states to compete in raising the quality of life of those they govern, it is among the most constructive forces in politics. When it pushes them to compete in exclusion, it becomes among the most destructive.

The difference turns on one line: the line between loving one's own people and believing them superior to others. That line can be drawn clearly. It was drawn in the Qur'anic teaching that peoples and tribes exist to know one another and that the noblest is the most righteous, and in the prophetic rejection of partisanship that supports one's own in wrongdoing. Many other traditions recognise the same principle in their own terms.

From this follows a practical test that can be applied to any country. A just nationalism treats all citizens as equal in law, whatever their ethnicity, faith or origin. It guarantees non-citizens who live and work within its borders the same basic protections and access to justice, and offers long-term residents a realistic path to full membership. It allows honest criticism and protects it through independent institutions, while restraining proven lies and incitement. It takes pride in its culture and values without claiming that its people are worth more than others. And it measures its success by the quality of life it delivers.

No country examined here meets this test in full. Some come close in particular respects; others are moving away from it. That is the purpose of the standard: not to declare winners, but to give citizens, governments and observers a common measure. Nations will continue to take pride in themselves. The question is whether that pride will be just.
